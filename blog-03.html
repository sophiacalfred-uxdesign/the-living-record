03 Defining the Problem Space

April 27th, 2026

When I last left off, we had 60 answers to the question "What is the function of the NHM gardens?" and five overarching themes to show for it. We had a broad, shared understanding of the problem space. But understanding something broadly and knowing what to do about it are very different things.

Ideation

To begin generating ideas, we tested out different ideation techniques. Sydney ran the critical thinking with AI exercise from class, and the outputs she shared allowed us to engage with perspectives we hadn't considered: missing stakeholders, underlying assumptions, and questions we hadn't thought to ask.

For further ideation, Tom encouraged us to try techniques beyond what we'd done in other modules. We decided on a brainwriting exercise. Unlike Crazy 8s, which is individual, brainwriting is collaborative and sequential. Each person started writing ideas on a piece of paper, then passed it to the next person after two minutes where that person would pick up where they left left off, building on or departing from their ideas. We did two full cycles (twelve minutes total), reviewed what we had, then ran a third focused cycle of six minutes to hone in on the stronger threads.

Brainwriting Exercise

I found it difficult to push myself to generate new ideas in such short bursts of time. However, the combination of my teammates' ideas already on the page and the time limit worked together to force divergent thinking. There was no time to stall or wait for ideas to come.

Clustering

After all three cycles, we put our ideas on sticky notes and clustered them by theme.

Brainwriting: Affinity Clusters

Categories emerged: digital twins, interactive experiences, data visualisation, VR. Sitting with those clusters, the ideas that excited us most were the ones that put visitors inside the data.

An exhibit. An immersive, interactive exhibit inside the Natural History Museum itself.

More on what that meant for our design direction in the next post.
